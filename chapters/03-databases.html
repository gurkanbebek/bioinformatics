2  From Biological Data to Biological Knowledge

2.1 Learning Objectives

After completing this chapter, students will be able to:

  • Explain why biological databases are critical for modern research.
  • Distinguish between Disease Gene Prioritization and Systems Description paradigms.
  • Describe the data-centric view of biology.
  • Understand the purpose of genome annotation.
  • Interpret common genomic features such as genes, variants, and regulatory elements.
  • Explain how genome browsers integrate biological knowledge.
  • Use genomic data to generate biological hypotheses.

2.2 The Challenge of Modern Biology

Imagine you have just completed an RNA sequencing experiment. After weeks of sample preparation, sequencing, quality control, and statistical analysis, you obtain a list of several hundred differentially expressed genes. Alternatively, perhaps you have performed whole-genome sequencing and identified thousands of genetic variants.

Now what?

The challenge facing modern biology is no longer simply generating data. Advances in sequencing, proteomics, and imaging technologies have enabled researchers to measure biological systems at an unprecedented scale. However, understanding what those measurements mean remains exceptionally difficult. A list of genes is not a biological explanation. Researchers must determine what these genes do, how they interact, whether they are associated with disease, which pathways they influence, and how they contribute to overall biological function. Transforming raw data into knowledge requires access to existing biological information and computational tools capable of integrating diverse sources of evidence.

2.3 Why Biological Databases Matter

Biological systems are inherently multidimensional. A single disease may involve genetic mutations, changes in RNA expression, altered protein abundance, epigenetic modifications, and environmental influences. Different experimental technologies measure different aspects of these systems. As a result, modern biology depends heavily on biological databases that organize and store knowledge generated over decades of research.

Biological information stored in databases includes gene locations, functional annotations, protein sequences, regulatory elements, disease associations, pathways, molecular interactions, and genetic variants. These databases serve as the collective memory of the scientific community. Without these resources, every investigator would need to rediscover what is already known.

2.4 Two Paradigms for Understanding Biological Systems

When researchers attempt to understand disease and biological function, they generally adopt one of two complementary perspectives.

2.4.1 Disease Gene Prioritization

The first paradigm asks which genes are most likely to contribute to a specific disease. In many genomic studies, researchers begin with hundreds or thousands of candidate genes, but only a small subset will have true biological relevance. Disease gene prioritization seeks to rank these candidates according to their likelihood of involvement in a phenotype or disease (Gill et al. 2014).

Evidence used for prioritization includes genetic association studies, functional annotations, protein interactions, pathway membership, evolutionary conservation, and computational predictions. The goal is to focus experimental effort on the most promising candidates. This approach is widely applied in disease gene discovery, biomarker identification, drug target development, and precision medicine. In essence, disease gene prioritization helps identify the most important molecular players.

2.4.2 Systems Description

The second paradigm asks how the biological system functions as a whole. Knowing which genes are important is only part of the story, as biological functions emerge from interactions among genes, proteins, metabolites, and cellular processes.

Systems description focuses on networks, pathways, regulatory circuits, molecular interactions, and emergent biological behavior. Rather than studying isolated components, systems biology seeks to understand how components operate together as a coordinated system to drive disease mechanisms and discover novel therapeutic targets. While disease gene prioritization identifies the players, systems description explains the rules of the game.

2.5 A Data-Centric View of Biology

Traditional biology is often described through the central dogma where DNA is transcribed into RNA, which is translated into protein. While conceptually useful, this representation is incomplete for modern omics. Modern biology operates through complex layers of interconnected information moving from DNA to genes, transcripts, proteins, pathways, phenotypes, and ultimately disease.

Each level generates its own forms of data and utilizes specialized databases. For example, DNA studies generate genetic variant data, RNA studies measure expression levels, and pathway analyses outline biological processes. As investigators, our primary task is to connect observations across these distinct biological layers.

2.6 Genome Annotation Adding Meaning to Sequence

A genome sequence by itself is simply a string of nucleotides. Without additional context, we cannot determine where genes are located, which regions are regulatory, which variants affect function, or which regions are evolutionarily conserved. This additional information is called genome annotation, which assigns biological meaning to genomic coordinates (Wang 2024).

Gene annotations identify structural features like gene boundaries, exons, introns, and untranslated regions. Regulatory annotations map promoters, enhancers, and transcription factor binding sites. Variation annotations identify single nucleotide polymorphisms, insertions, deletions, and broader structural variants. Finally, comparative annotations identify conserved regions and orthologous genes across species to establish evolutionary relationships. Through these processes, annotation transforms raw sequence into biological knowledge (Prosdocimi et al. 2020).

2.7 Genome Browsers as Knowledge Integration Platforms

A common misconception is that genome browsers are themselves databases. A genome browser is actually better viewed as a visualization and integration platform. Genome browsers combine information from many disparate databases and display it within a shared genomic coordinate system (Figure 2.4).

Think of a genome browser as a map for the genome. The reference genome serves as the underlying geography, while individual annotation tracks represent different layers of information. Researchers can simultaneously view genes, variants, disease associations, regulatory elements, expression data, and conservation scores to understand how different pieces of biological information relate to one another in physical space.

2.7.1 The UCSC Genome Browser

One of the most widely used platforms is the UCSC Genome Browser (Nassar et al. 2022). It provides access to thousands of annotation tracks organized into logical categories. The genes and gene prediction tracks display RefSeq genes, UCSC curated genes, and transcript evidence. Variation tracks visualize single nucleotide polymorphisms, structural variants, and population frequencies. Regulation tracks highlight promoters, enhancers, and transcription factor binding sites. Comparative genomics tracks allow researchers to view cross-species alignments and conservation scores, while phenotype and disease tracks map known clinical variants. Because these annotations share common genomic coordinates, relationships between different data types become immediately visible to the researcher.

2.8 Interpreting Genomic Context

One of the most important skills in genomics is interpreting biological context. When a researcher discovers a genetic variant, they must determine if it is biologically important. The answer depends heavily on its context.

Critical questions include whether the variant occurs inside a coding gene, if it alters a protein sequence, if it falls within a regulatory promoter, whether the region is evolutionarily conserved, and whether it has been previously linked to disease. Genome browsers enable researchers to gather evidence from multiple sources simultaneously, demonstrating that true biological interpretation emerges from data integration.

2.9 Chapter Summary

Modern biological research depends on our ability to transform large-scale data into meaningful knowledge.

Two complementary paradigms guide this process:

  1. Disease Gene Prioritization identifies genes most relevant to disease.
  2. Systems Description reveals how biological components interact within larger systems.

Genome annotation adds meaning to raw DNA sequence by identifying genes, variants, regulatory elements, and evolutionary features. Genome browsers then integrate these diverse annotations into a unified visual framework, enabling researchers to investigate biological questions and generate new hypotheses.

Ultimately, modern genomics is not simply about collecting data. It is about connecting information across multiple levels of biology to gain insight into how living systems function.
