3  Ontologies, Databases, and Statistical Foundations

Author

Gurkan Bebek, Ph.D., M.S.

3.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, readers should be able to:

  • Differentiate between ontologies and functional annotations.
  • Explain how Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) organize biological knowledge.
  • Describe the three domains of the Gene Ontology.
  • Apply the True Path Rule to interpret biological annotations.
  • Navigate major biological databases and understand common identifiers.
  • Interpret Gene Ontology evidence codes.
  • Explain the rationale behind functional enrichment analysis.
  • Understand the foundations of Over-Representation Analysis (ORA) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA).
  • Apply principles of multiple-testing correction using FWER and FDR.
  • Recognize common pitfalls in enrichment analysis.

3.2 Introduction: Organizing the Biological Universe

Modern high-throughput technologies generate enormous amounts of biological data. RNA sequencing experiments routinely measure the expression of tens of thousands of genes, while proteomic and metabolomic studies can quantify thousands of molecular features simultaneously. The result is often a long list of statistically significant genes, proteins, or metabolites.

However, biological discovery does not emerge from the list itself. A list of 500 differentially expressed genes does not immediately explain a disease process, reveal a therapeutic target, or provide mechanistic insight. The central challenge of systems biology is therefore transforming large-scale molecular measurements into meaningful biological interpretations (Hood and Tian 2012).

Three foundational resources make this possible:

  1. Biomedical ontologies, which provide standardized vocabularies for describing biological concepts and their relationships.
  2. Curated biological databases, which organize and distribute biological knowledge using stable identifiers and standardized formats.
  3. Statistical frameworks, which determine whether biological functions or pathways are represented more frequently than expected by chance.

Together, these resources allow researchers to move from molecular measurements to biological understanding.

3.3 Biomedical Ontologies

3.3.1 Ontology vs. Annotation

One of the most important distinctions in bioinformatics is the difference between an ontology and an annotation.

An ontology defines biological concepts and the relationships among them. It serves as a structured representation of knowledge that remains independent of any specific organism or experiment.

An annotation links a biological entity, such as a gene or protein, to a concept within that ontology.

       +-------------------------------------------------------+
       |                     THE ONTOLOGY                      |
       |  Controlled Vocabulary + Graph Hierarchy (e.g., DAG)  |
       |     [Apoptosis] <--(is_a)-- [Programmed Cell Death]   |
       +-------------------------------------------------------+
                                   ^
                                   | Annotation
                                   |
       +-------------------------------------------------------+
       |                    GENE PRODUCTS                      |
       |             UniProt: P04637 (Human TP53)              |
       |             Ensembl: ENSG00000141510                  |
       +-------------------------------------------------------+

A useful analogy is that an ontology functions like a dictionary and organizational map, whereas annotations are the entries connecting specific biological entities to terms in that dictionary.

For example, the term apoptotic process exists in the Gene Ontology regardless of any particular gene. When curators determine that TP53 participates in apoptosis, they create an annotation linking TP53 to that term.

3.4 Why Biological Knowledge Requires Hierarchies

Biological concepts are naturally hierarchical. Cell division is a type of cellular process. Mitosis is a type of cell division. Chromosome segregation is a component of mitosis.

An effective biological knowledge system must therefore capture relationships among concepts while allowing investigators to move between broad and specific descriptions.

The Gene Ontology achieves this using a graph-based structure known as a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG).

3.4.1 Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs)

A graph consists of:

  • Nodes, representing biological concepts.
  • Edges, representing relationships between concepts.

Unlike a simple tree, where each node has only one parent, a DAG allows multiple parent terms (Ashburner et al. 2000).

This design reflects biological reality. A process such as apoptosis may simultaneously belong to multiple higher-level biological categories. A simple tree cannot adequately represent these overlapping relationships.

The “acyclic” property prevents loops. If a researcher follows parent-child relationships upward through the ontology, they will never return to the same term.

3.5 The Gene Ontology

Developed by the Gene Ontology Consortium, GO provides a standardized vocabulary for describing gene product properties across organisms (Ashburner et al. 2000; Gene Ontology Consortium 2021).

GO is organized into three independent domains.

3.5.1 Biological Process (BP)

A Biological Process describes a series of coordinated events that accomplish a biological objective.

Examples include:

  • Cell cycle
  • Immune response
  • Signal transduction
  • Apoptosis

Processes typically involve multiple molecular functions operating together.

3.5.2 Molecular Function (MF)

A Molecular Function describes the fundamental biochemical activity of a gene product.

Examples include:

  • ATP binding
  • Protein kinase activity
  • DNA binding

Molecular function answers the question:

What does this molecule do?

3.5.3 Cellular Component (CC)

A Cellular Component describes where a gene product functions.

Examples include:

  • Nucleus
  • Plasma membrane
  • Ribosome
  • Mitochondrial matrix

Cellular component answers the question:

Where does it act?

3.6 Relationship Types and Biological Meaning

GO terms are connected through well-defined semantic relationships.

The most important relationships are:

  • is_a: Represents subclass membership.
  • part_of: Represents structural or functional inclusion.

For example:

protein kinase activity
         is_a
kinase activity

and

nucleus
      part_of
eukaryotic cell

These relationships allow computational tools to navigate biological knowledge in a consistent and meaningful manner.

3.7 The True Path Rule

A central principle of GO is the True Path Rule (Ashburner et al. 2000; Rhee et al. 2008).

If a gene is annotated to a specific term,
it is automatically annotated to all ancestral terms.

For example:

negative regulation of apoptosis
                |
                v
regulation of apoptosis
                |
                v
cell death
                |
                v
biological process

If TP53 is annotated to negative regulation of apoptosis, it is implicitly associated with all higher-level categories above it.

This rule provides consistency throughout the ontology and allows researchers to analyze biological functions at multiple levels of granularity.

However, it also introduces redundancy during enrichment analysis because parent categories inherit genes from their descendants.

3.8 Biological Databases and Data Standards

Ontologies define biological concepts, but biological databases provide the information needed to connect genes and proteins to those concepts.

Several resources play central roles in modern bioinformatics.

Database Primary Focus Identifier
UniProtKB Protein sequence and function UniProt Accession
IntAct Molecular interactions IntAct ID
PANTHER Protein families and pathways PANTHER ID
NCBI Gene Gene-centric information Entrez Gene ID

These resources are heavily cross-linked and form an interconnected ecosystem of biological knowledge.

3.8.1 Gene Association Files (GAF)

Gene Ontology annotations are commonly distributed using the Gene Association Format (GAF).

Each annotation typically contains:

  • Gene identifier
  • GO identifier
  • Ontology category
  • Evidence code
  • Reference source

GAF files serve as the foundation for most enrichment-analysis software.

3.8.2 Evidence Codes

Not all GO annotations are supported by the same level of evidence.

Code Meaning
EXP Inferred from experiment
IDA Inferred from direct assay
IPI Inferred from physical interaction
ISS Inferred from sequence similarity
IEA Inferred from electronic annotation

Evidence codes help researchers evaluate annotation reliability.

WarningInterpreting Electronic Annotations

Many GO annotations carry the IEA evidence code and are assigned automatically using computational pipelines. These annotations are extremely valuable for large-scale analyses, but experimentally supported annotations are generally considered more reliable when detailed biological interpretation is required.

3.9 Statistical Foundations of Functional Enrichment

After identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs), researchers often ask:

Which biological functions are associated with these genes?

This question motivates functional enrichment analysis.

3.9.1 Why Raw Counts Are Not Enough

Large biological categories naturally contain many genes. Consequently, categories with more genes frequently appear near the top of ranked lists even when they are not biologically relevant.

ImportantThe Mitosis vs. Glucose Transport Example

Suppose an experiment measures 2,000 genes and identifies 200 DEGs.

Among them:

  • Mitosis: 160 DEGs
  • Glucose Transport: 40 DEGs

At first glance, mitosis appears more important.

However, if the experiment includes 1,600 mitotic genes, then observing 160 DEGs is exactly what would be expected by chance.

If only 100 genes are involved in glucose transport, observing 40 DEGs instead of an expected 10 represents substantial enrichment.

The key question is therefore not:

How many genes are present?

but rather:

How many genes are present relative to expectation?

3.10 Over-Representation Analysis (ORA)

ORA evaluates whether a biological category contains more genes than expected by chance.

For a GO term (T), we construct a contingency table:

Category In Term T Not in Term T
DEGs k n-k
Non-DEGs M-k Remaining

where:

  • (N) = total background genes
  • (M) = genes associated with the term
  • (n) = total DEGs
  • (k) = DEGs associated with the term

The significance of this overlap is typically assessed using Fisher’s Exact Test.

NoteMathematical Foundation

ORA is based on the hypergeometric distribution:

\[ P(X=k)= \frac{\binom{M}{k}\binom{N-M}{n-k}} {\binom{N}{n}} \]

which models sampling without replacement from a finite population.

ORA remains one of the most widely used methods in functional genomics because it is intuitive, computationally efficient, and statistically robust.

3.11 Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

ORA requires a threshold that separates significant and non-significant genes.

This creates an artificial boundary. Genes just above and below the cutoff are often biologically similar.

To address this issue, Subramanian and colleagues developed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) (Subramanian et al. 2005).

Rank all genes
       ↓
Walk through ranked list
       ↓
Calculate enrichment score
       ↓
Estimate significance using permutations

Rather than analyzing only selected DEGs, GSEA evaluates the entire ranked dataset.

A pathway is considered enriched if its member genes cluster near the top or bottom of the ranked list more often than expected by chance.

Because GSEA uses all measured genes, it is often better at detecting subtle but coordinated biological changes.

3.11.1 Beyond Independent GO Terms

GO categories are not independent because of the True Path Rule and hierarchical structure.

Consequently, many related terms may appear significant simultaneously.

Specialized topology-aware methods have been developed to reduce redundancy and better account for GO structure (Alexa et al. 2006). These approaches help distinguish truly specific biological signals from inherited parental effects.

3.12 Multiple Hypothesis Testing

A typical enrichment analysis evaluates hundreds or thousands of GO terms simultaneously.

If 10,000 tests are performed using a significance threshold of 0.05, approximately 500 false positives are expected by chance alone.

3.12.1 Family-Wise Error Rate and Bonferroni Correction

The Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER) represents the probability of making at least one false-positive discovery.

Bonferroni correction controls this error rate by dividing the significance threshold by the number of tests performed:

\[ \alpha_{adjusted}=\frac{\alpha}{m} \]

While effective, Bonferroni correction can be overly conservative in genomic studies.

3.12.2 False Discovery Rate (FDR)

Modern genomics typically relies on the False Discovery Rate (FDR) (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995).

Rather than eliminating all false positives, FDR controls the expected proportion of false discoveries among reported findings.

An FDR threshold of 0.05 implies that approximately 5% of significant results are expected to be false positives.

This balance between sensitivity and reliability makes FDR the preferred correction strategy for most high-throughput analyses.

3.13 Common Pitfalls in Functional Enrichment Analysis

3.13.1 The Background Universe

The most common error in enrichment analysis is choosing an inappropriate background.

WarningUse the Correct Background

If an experiment measured 1,000 genes, then those 1,000 genes should define the statistical universe.

Using all genes in the genome as the background can dramatically inflate significance and generate misleading biological conclusions.

The background should always reflect the set of genes that could realistically have been detected.

3.13.2 Biological Cross-Talk

Genes frequently participate in multiple pathways.

Consequently, enrichment results often contain clusters of related biological terms derived from overlapping genes.

Researchers should interpret these categories collectively rather than viewing every enriched term as an independent biological discovery.

3.13.3 Ignoring Annotation Quality

The quality of a biological interpretation depends on the quality of its underlying annotations.

Understanding evidence codes and annotation provenance helps researchers distinguish well-supported findings from tentative computational predictions.

3.14 Chapter Summary

Modern systems biology depends on structured biological knowledge, curated databases, and rigorous statistical methods.

  • Ontologies provide standardized representations of biological concepts.
  • The Gene Ontology organizes knowledge using Directed Acyclic Graphs.
  • The True Path Rule propagates annotations upward through the ontology hierarchy.
  • Databases such as UniProt, IntAct, PANTHER, and NCBI Gene provide curated biological information.
  • GO annotations are distributed through standardized formats and supported by evidence codes.
  • ORA evaluates enrichment relative to expectation using Fisher’s Exact Test and the hypergeometric framework.
  • GSEA evaluates ranked gene lists without requiring arbitrary thresholds.
  • Multiple-testing correction, particularly FDR, is essential for reliable interpretation.
  • Careful selection of background populations and thoughtful interpretation remain critical for meaningful biological conclusions.
