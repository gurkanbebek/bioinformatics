4  RNA-Seq Analysis Pipeline

Author

Gurkan Bebek, Ph.D., M.S.

4.1 Introduction

RNA Sequencing (RNA-Seq) has revolutionized our ability to study the transcriptome. By taking advantage of high-throughput sequencing technologies, we can quantify gene expression, discover novel splice variants, and identify differential expression between biological conditions (Conesa et al. 2016). This chapter covers the fundamental technical details of a standard reference-based RNA-Seq pipeline, guiding you through raw data handling, quality control, read mapping, and downstream statistical analysis.

4.2 RNA Isolation and Library Preparation

Before sequencing can occur, cellular RNA must be converted into a sequencing-ready library. Library preparation is a critical experimental step because choices made at this stage directly influence downstream analysis and interpretation.

A typical RNA-seq library preparation workflow consists of several steps that transform biological RNA molecules into DNA fragments compatible with next-generation sequencing platforms.

4.2.1 RNA Extraction

The process begins with the isolation of total RNA from cells, tissues, or biological fluids. RNA quality is commonly assessed using metrics such as the RNA Integrity Number (RIN), which measures the degree of RNA degradation.

High-quality RNA is essential because degraded samples can lead to biased transcript quantification and reduced detection of full-length transcripts. Such errors can lead to false-postives in downstream statistical analysis.

4.2.2 RNA Selection or Depletion

Most total RNA consists of ribosomal RNA (rRNA), which typically represents more than 80% of cellular RNA. Because rRNA is generally not informative for gene expression studies, it is either removed or avoided through enrichment strategies.

Two common approaches are:

  • Poly(A) Selection: Enriches mature messenger RNAs by capturing transcripts containing polyadenylated tails.
  • rRNA Depletion: Removes ribosomal RNA while retaining both coding and non-coding transcripts.

The choice depends on the research question and sample type. Poly(A) selection is widely used for standard gene expression studies, whereas rRNA depletion is preferred when studying non-coding RNAs, degraded samples, or archival specimens.

4.2.3 cDNA Synthesis

Because most sequencing platforms are designed to sequence DNA rather than RNA, isolated RNA molecules must first be converted into complementary DNA (cDNA).

During this process:

  1. Reverse transcriptase generates a first-strand cDNA molecule from the RNA template.
  2. A second DNA strand is synthesized to create double-stranded cDNA suitable for library construction.

4.2.4 Fragmentation and Adapter Ligation

The resulting cDNA molecules are fragmented into sizes compatible with sequencing instruments, typically ranging from 200 to 500 base pairs.

Synthetic adapter sequences are then ligated to both ends of each fragment. These adapters contain:

  • Sequencing primer binding sites.
  • Flow-cell attachment sequences.
  • Sample-specific barcode sequences for multiplexing.

These adapter sequences later become important during quality control because any portion of an adapter that is sequenced must be identified and removed computationally.

4.2.5 PCR Amplification

Following adapter ligation, libraries are amplified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to generate sufficient material for sequencing.

While amplification increases library yield, excessive PCR cycling can introduce:

  • Amplification bias.
  • Uneven transcript representation.
  • Duplicate sequencing reads.

Consequently, modern protocols attempt to minimize PCR amplification whenever possible.

4.2.6 Sequencing and FASTQ Generation

The final library consists of adapter-flanked cDNA fragments representing the original RNA population. These libraries are loaded onto a sequencing instrument, where millions of fragments are sequenced in parallel.

The sequencer ultimately produces the raw data files used in bioinformatics analysis: FASTQ files, which contain the nucleotide sequences and associated quality scores for every read.

Note

Why Library Preparation Matters

Many characteristics observed during downstream quality control originate from decisions made during library preparation:

Adapter contamination results from sequencing into adapter sequences. Duplicate reads can arise during PCR amplification. Transcript representation depends on whether poly(A) selection or rRNA depletion was used. Strand specificity is determined by the library preparation protocol. Coverage bias can be introduced during fragmentation, reverse transcription, or amplification.

For this reason, understanding library preparation is essential for correctly interpreting RNA-seq results.

4.3 The FASTQ Format

When RNA-Seq libraries are sequenced, the raw data is returned in a standard text-based format known as FASTQ. A FASTQ file stores both the biological sequence and its corresponding quality scores. The following is sample FASTQ entry:

TipExample 
@HWUSI-EAS611:34:6669YAAXX:1:1:5069:1159 1:N:0:
TCGATAATACCGTTTTTTTCCGTTTGATGTTGATACCATT
+
IIHIIHIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIHIIIIHIIIII

Each sequence read in a FASTQ file is represented by four lines:

  1. Sequence Identifier: Begins with an @ symbol and contains information about the sequencing run and cluster location.
  2. Raw Sequence: The actual nucleotide sequence (A, C, G, T, and N for unknown bases).
  3. Separator: A + sign, sometimes followed by a repetition of the sequence identifier.
  4. Quality Scores: Characters encoding the quality of each base call in the sequence (must contain the exact same number of characters as line 2).

4.3.1 Q-Values and Quality Scores

The characters in the fourth line of a FASTQ file represent the Phred Quality Score (\(Q\)-score) for each base. The \(Q\)-score is logarithmically related to the probability of an incorrect base call (\(P\)).

The formula to calculate the \(Q\)-value is:

\[Q = -10 \log_{10}(P)\]

For example, if the probability of an incorrect base call is 1 in 100 (\(P = 0.01\)), the \(Q\)-score is:

\[Q = -10 \log_{10}(0.01) = 20\]

A \(Q\)-score of 30 (\(P = 0.001\)) means a base call accuracy of 99.9%, which is generally considered the benchmark for high-quality sequencing data.

4.3.2 ASCII Conversion of Quality Scores

To save space, \(Q\)-scores are not written as two-digit numbers in the FASTQ file. Instead, they are mapped to standard ASCII characters. Because early ASCII characters (0–32) include non-printable control characters (like space or tab), a constant is added to the \(Q\)-score to shift it into the printable character range.

In modern Illumina sequencing, the Phred+33 encoding is used. The formula for conversion is:

\[\text{ASCII} = Q + 33\]

For a \(Q\)-score of 30, the ASCII value is \(30 + 33 = 63\). Looking at an ASCII table, the value 63 corresponds to the ? character.

4.3.3 Sequencing Coverage Calculation

When assessing RNA-Seq output, it is crucial to understand your coverage (or read depth). Coverage refers to the average number of times a given base in the transcriptome has been sequenced.

The theoretical coverage (\(C\)) can be calculated using the Lander-Waterman equation:

\[C = \frac{N \times L}{G}\]

Where:

  • \(N\): Total number of reads generated.
  • \(L\): Average read length (in base pairs).
  • \(G\): Size of the target genome or transcriptome (in base pairs).
TipExample

Suppose you sequenced a human RNA-seq sample and obtained \(34\) million raw reads with an average read length of \(100\) bp. If your alignment success rate is \(90\%\), the number of successfully mapped, usable reads (\(N\)) is \(34,000,000 \times .90 = 30,600,000\) .

For human RNA-seq, we typically estimate the size of the target expressed transcriptome (\(G\)) to be roughly 100 Megabases (100,000,000 bp).

Using the Lander-Waterman equation:

\[C = \frac{30,600,000 \times 100}{100,000,000} = 30.6\]

This means that, on average, each base in the expressed human transcriptome is covered by approximately \(30\) reads.

Note💡 Key Concept: Exome vs. Transcriptome

The exome is a static blueprint of DNA coding regions, whereas the transcriptome is the dynamic, cell-specific readout of RNA molecules. They overlap heavily in content, but they are not the same thing.

4.4 Direct Comparison

Feature The Human Exome The Human Transcriptome
Primary Molecule DNA (Genomic DNA) RNA (Transcripts, often sequenced as cDNA)
Physical Footprint ~30 to 60 Megabases (Mb) (~1% to 2% of the total genome) ~100 to 140 Mb (Total annotated non-redundant transcript space)
Temporal Nature Static. It remains identical across almost every cell type in an organism. Dynamic. It constantly shifts based on cell type, developmental stage, and environmental stimuli.
Inclusions Strictly exons from the ~20,000–23,000 protein-coding genes. Exons from expressed genes plus non-coding RNAs (e.g., lncRNA, miRNA, tRNA, rRNA).
Analytical Scope Tells you what protein variations an organism could potentially make. Tells you what genes an organism is actively using at a specific moment.

4.5 The Reference-Based RNA-Seq Pipeline

A standard reference-based RNA-Seq analysis involves several sequential steps, transitioning from raw data to downstream statistical interpretation.

4.6 Raw Sequence Quality Control Process

Before aligning the data, the raw sequences must be computationally assessed to ensure high quality. This process involves scanning the FASTQ files to calculate aggregate statistics.

During this step, the pipeline evaluates:

  • Per-base sequence quality: Ensuring the average \(Q\)-score remains high across the entire length of the reads.
  • Sequence bias and GC content: Checking for deviations from expected distributions. Significant biases in sequence content at the beginning of reads are often artifacts of random hexamer priming during cDNA synthesis (Hansen et al. 2010).
  • Duplication levels: High duplication rates can indicate PCR amplification artifacts or optical duplicates.
  • Adapter Content: Identifying if sequencing proceeded past the biological insert and read into the synthetic adapter sequences.

4.7 Adapter and Quality Trimming Process

If the quality control step identifies adapter contamination or poor-quality base calls (often at the 3’ end of the reads), the sequences must be trimmed.

The trimming process algorithmically searches for known adapter sequences and removes them. Additionally, a sliding window approaches the ends of the reads, dropping bases that fall below a designated \(Q\)-score threshold (e.g., \(Q < 20\)). Reads that become too short after trimming are entirely discarded to prevent them from mapping ambiguously to the genome.

4.8 Read Mapping (Alignment)

Because eukaryotic RNA is spliced (introns are removed before mature mRNA is formed), mapping RNA-Seq reads back to a reference genome requires a splice-aware aligner. Standard DNA aligners drop reads that span across two exons because they appear to contain massive gaps relative to the genomic reference.

Splice-aware aligners utilize a graph FM index and require a set of known splice sites to accurately map reads across these exon-exon junctions (Kim et al. 2015).

In a standard Linux environment, the alignment process typically looks like this:

# 1. Generate the genome index from reference FastA and GTF annotation
STAR --runMode genomeGenerate \
     --genomeDir yeast_star_index \
     --genomeFastaFiles yeast_genome.fa \
     --sjdbGTFfile yeast_gene_models.gtf \
     --runThreadN 8

# 2. Map reads and directly generate a coordinate-sorted BAM file
STAR --runThreadN 8 \
     --genomeDir yeast_star_index \
     --readFilesIn sample.fq.gz \
     --readFilesCommand zcat \
     --outSAMtype BAM SortedByCoordinate \
     --outFileNamePrefix yeast_sample

We first generate an index of the genome. This is done once, and you can use the same index for multiple times. In this process gene locations are provided for splice aware alignment. This is followed by alignment of the sequences we have to the exome.

4.9 Downstream Analysis

Once the data is mapped and stored as a BAM file, we proceed to downstream analysis, which transitions raw alignments into biological insights.

4.9.1 Visualization and Custom Tracks

Visualizing quantitative read coverage over genomic regions (often done via “wiggle plots”) helps identify features such as local read depth variations or unexpected antisense transcription (Gertz et al. 2012).

At this stage, researchers often create custom annotation tracks to focus their analysis. For instance, filtering the annotation to include only protein_coding genes or excluding mitochondrial (MT) chromosomes ensures that the subsequent statistical tests are focused strictly on regions of interest.

4.9.2 Pairwise Comparison and Differential Expression

To find statistically significant changes between biological groups (e.g., untreated control vs. treated samples), we generate uncorrected raw read counts for each genomic feature.

Tools designed for differential expression analyze these counts using specialized statistical models (such as the negative binomial distribution) to find outliers from the general distribution (Love et al. 2014). This process accounts for intensity-dependent noise—where highly expressed genes naturally show higher variance—and applies normalization factors to correct for differences in sequencing depth across samples. The output is a robust list of transcripts showing a statistically significant fold-change (e.g., \(p\text{-value} < 0.05\)).

4.9.3 Multi-Comparison and Clustering

Once a list of significantly differentially expressed genes is generated, the next step is discovering shared patterns of expression across multiple conditions or time points.

  • Hierarchical Clustering: This visual method rearranges genes so that transcripts with similar quantitative profiles are placed adjacent to one another, forming distinct blocks or “heatmaps” of co-regulated genes.
  • Manual Correlation Filtering: In time-course experiments, researchers can manually define specific expression profiles (e.g., identifying genes whose expression steadily increases over time). The clustering tool then searches the dataset for any transcripts that heavily correlate (e.g., \(R > 0.9\)) with that specific pattern, facilitating the discovery of biologically relevant gene networks.

4.10 Chapter Summary

This chapter outlines the complete technical workflow for a standard reference-based RNA-Seq analysis, bridging the gap between raw sequencing files and biological discovery:

  • Data Standards & Metrics: Details raw FASTQ file structures, the logarithmic math behind Phred \(Q\)-scores (\(Q = -10 \log_{10}(P)\)), ASCII Phred+33 offset conversions, and Lander-Waterman coverage calculations (\(C = \frac{N \times L}{G}\)).
  • Quality Control & Trimming: Outlines initial data diagnostics (per-base quality, GC content bias, duplication, and adapter contamination) alongside sliding-window trimming algorithms that discard low-quality tails and short fragments.
  • Splice-Aware Alignment: Addresses the challenge of eukaryotic intron splicing and demonstrates how graph-based aligners (e.g., HISAT2) map reads across exon-exon junctions using reference annotations and genome indexing.
  • Downstream Statistical Analysis: Covers read coverage visualization, feature filtering, differential expression testing using negative binomial modeling, and multi-condition profile discovery via hierarchical clustering and correlation filtering (\(R > 0.9\)).

4.11 Key Terms and Keywords

  • RNA-Seq Pipeline: The sequential computational protocol for quality checking, aligning, and quantifying transcriptomic sequencing data.
  • FASTQ Format: The standard four-line text format containing sequence identifiers, raw nucleotide bases, separators, and quality scores.
  • Phred Quality Score (\(Q\)-score): A logarithmic measure of base-calling error probability; \(Q \ge 30\) (99.9% accuracy) serves as the standard benchmark.
  • Phred+33 Encoding: The standard ASCII mapping method that adds 33 to a \(Q\)-score to represent quality values as printable characters.
  • Lander-Waterman Equation: The mathematical formula (\(C = \frac{N \times L}{G}\)) used to calculate theoretical sequence coverage depth.
  • Splice-Aware Aligner: An alignment tool (such as HISAT2) capable of splitting reads across introns to map to non-contiguous genomic exons.
  • Differential Expression (DE): Statistical hypothesis testing using negative binomial distributions to detect significant fold-changes in gene expression between conditions.
  • Hierarchical Clustering: An unsupervised grouping method that arranges co-expressed genes into visual heatmaps based on expression profile similarity.
